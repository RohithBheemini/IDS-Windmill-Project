1. INTRODUCTION AND PROBLEM STATEMENT

Commercial wind turbines are massive mechanical power plants standing over 300 feet tall, operating in harsh onshore and offshore environments. Each turbine captures kinetic wind energy across three 80-meter blades turning at 8 to 20 revolutions per minute (RPM). An internal 20-ton mechanical gearbox multiplies this speed by 1:90 to 1,500 RPM for the generator. High-voltage transformers step generated electricity from 690 V to 33 kV for grid transmission.

When an uptower component suffers sudden failure—such as a gearbox bearing shattering inside Turbine #21—the financial consequences are catastrophic. Mobilizing a specialized Jack-Up Offshore Crane Vessel costs between $25,000 and $40,000 per day. If winter waves prevent the vessel from docking, the turbine sits idle for 6 to 8 weeks, losing hundreds of thousands of dollars in green energy revenue on top of a $150,000 replacement bill. Total losses easily exceed $250,000 to $350,000 per breakdown [4].

An anomaly score alone is not actionable. If an automated system merely reports that a turbine is at “90% risk,” engineers cannot act unless they know which component is breaking and why. This case study addresses two core challenges: (1) Prediction & Earliness: How accurately and how many weeks in advance can impending breakdowns be detected from SCADA telemetry without data leakage or synthetic points? (2) Explanation & Trust: Which physical sensor channels are driving the alert, and does the mathematical attribution match real mechanical degradation?

2. OBJECTIVES

  1. To clean and prepare the CARE_To_Compare SCADA benchmark in R, resolving missing values and strictly eliminating post-trip status code leakage.
  2. To engineer physics-first features: thermodynamic differential temperatures (ΔT = TComponent − TAmbient), causal 6-hour backward rolling fevers, and gearbox transmission slip ratios.
  3. To benchmark Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost under Leave-One-Turbine-Out Group Cross-Validation.
  4. To evaluate early warning lead times under the CARE Earliness framework and explain predictions using TreeSHAP and LIME.
  5. To tune decision thresholds against a 40:1 asymmetric cost matrix and deploy through an interactive R Shiny dashboard.

4. DATA PREPARATION AND CLEANING

4.1 Problems Detected & Anti-Patterns Avoided

Auditing the raw SCADA telemetry files exposed four critical engineering and methodological challenges. Table 4.1 summarizes each problem along with our implemented solution.

Table 4.1   Methodological defects detected and resolutions implemented
No. Methodological Problem Operational Danger in Practice Engineering Resolution
1 Status Code Leakage
(status_type_id == 4)		 Model learns to predict failure only after the safety controller has already tripped. Artificial 99.9% accuracy with zero advance notice. Strictly erased status_type_id and post-breakdown rows from predictive features. Evaluated only running states.
2 Synthetic Sampling
(Veto on SMOTE)		 SMOTE hallucinates physically impossible states (e.g. 2,000 kW at 0 m/s wind), destroying aerodynamic and thermodynamic validity. Zero synthetic data. Retained 100% genuine sensor telemetry, preserving real physical relations.
3 Quirk Memorization
(Asset ID Bias)		 Models trained with random splits memorize individual turbine baselines and fail on newly installed machines. Purged asset_id from features; enforced Leave-One-Turbine-Out Group Cross-Validation.
4 Sparse Sensor Gaps
(<0.00004% missing)		 Momentary SCADA communication dropouts produce sporadic NA entries in time series. Forward-filled missing intervals using Last Observation Carried Forward (zoo::na.locf).

4.2 Physics-First Feature Engineering (The “Rising Fever” ΔT)

In predictive maintenance, raw temperature is a deeply flawed indicator. On a 38°C summer afternoon, a healthy bearing operates at 60°C; on a 2°C winter night, a failing bearing suffering severe surface spalling may only reach 45°C. If a model inspects only raw temperatures, it misinterprets summer weather as an emergency and misses winter breakdowns.

To solve this, we formulated the Thermodynamic Temperature Differential, which mimics a doctor measuring fever above baseline ambient weather:

ΔTComponent = TComponent − TAmbient

Subtracting ambient temperature strips away seasonal weather fluctuations and cleanly exposes internal mechanical friction. To capture slow thermal heat accumulation without introducing look-ahead bias, we computed a causal 6-hour backward moving average (RollMean 6h) across 36 ten-minute timesteps:

T̄6h(t) = (1 / 36) ∑i=0..35 T(t − i · 10 min)

In addition, we engineered the Gearbox Transmission Slip Ratio (gen_rpm / rotor_rpm), which tracks mechanical drag or teeth slippage, and an Aerodynamic Power Proxy (active_power / (wind_speed³ + ε)) to monitor aerodynamic conversion degradation. The cleaned feature dataset holds 323,567 observations across 22 engineered predictors, requiring only 109 MB of RAM.

7. PREDICTION MODEL

7.1 Method and Justification (Why XGBoost Wins)

We benchmarked three model families representing linear, bagging, and boosting paradigms:

  • Logistic Regression (GLM): Linear log-odds baseline. While simple, it cannot capture non-linear aerodynamic power curves or multi-sensor thermal interactions.
  • Random Forest (ranger) [8]: An ensemble of 100 de-correlated decision trees (mtry = 4). It captures non-linear interactions well but requires significant memory and slower training.
  • Extreme Gradient Boosting (xgboost) [5]: Gradient boosted trees optimizing binary logistic loss. XGBoost was selected as the production architecture because it handles non-linear boundaries effortlessly, focuses sequentially on subtle thermal drifts, trains in sub-second speed, and integrates seamlessly with TreeSHAP.

8.3 Local Explanation with TreeSHAP and LIME

To ensure maintenance engineers trust the model, we integrated TreeSHAP [6] and LIME [7] to audit local feature attributions against ground-truth failure work orders.

Figure 6: TreeSHAP local feature attribution waterfall for Event 72. High-speed shaft bearing temperature (+0.9069) and lubricant differential (+0.4329) dominate the risk score, matching physical friction.

As demonstrated in Figure 6, for Event 72 (catastrophic gearbox bearing breakdown), the top TreeSHAP contributor is temp_gearbox_bearing (+0.9069 Shapley points), followed by delta_t_oil (+0.4329). In Event 0 (Generator bearing failure), Drive-End bearing temperature surges to 76–94°C, driving 82% of the local attribution. In healthy Event 25, all component scores remain negative. The model functions as an automated physical diagnostic tool.

8.4 Asymmetric Cost Economics & Fleet ROI (~$4.5M Net Savings)

In industrial maintenance, missing a breakdown is over 40 times more expensive than dispatching an engineer on a false alarm ($150,000 vs $3,500). By minimizing total operational costs across all outcomes:

Total Cost = (FN × $150,000) + (FP × $3,500) + (TP × $8,000)

we established that the optimal alarm threshold occurs at 0.30 to 0.35. Scaled across a commercial fleet of 36 turbines over 2 to 3 years (~45 major events avoided), the system yields approximately $4.5 Million in net fleet savings, avoids 6,500 hours of generation downtime, and achieves an estimated ROI exceeding 200%. All financial calculations explicitly cast counters to 64-bit float (as.numeric()) in R, guarding against 32-bit integer overflow.

9. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE

This case study built a complete industrial condition monitoring pipeline from raw 10-minute SCADA data to explainable web-based decision support. By respecting physical principles—eliminating post-trip status codes, rejecting synthetic SMOTE data, and formulating thermodynamic differentials (ΔT)—our production XGBoost classifier achieved 0.83 ROC-AUC on completely unseen turbines in just 0.67 seconds of training.

The system provides 8.0 to 34.2 days of advance notice before catastrophic trip, verified through TreeSHAP to reflect true mechanical degradation. Tuning the decision threshold to 0.35 unlocks ~$4.5M in net savings across 36 turbines. Two honest engineering limitations qualify these results: 10-minute SCADA intervals cannot capture high-frequency bearing vibration frequencies (kHz), and cross-farm geographic differences require domain adaptation.

Future Scope: (1) Embedding Navier-Stokes and heat diffusion equations into Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs); (2) Integrating 10-kHz accelerometer streams for bearing fault frequency demodulation (BPFO/BPFI); (3) Implementing domain-adversarial transfer learning across diverse offshore wind farms.
