1. The Physical Reality: Windmills & The $150,000 Crisis

1.1 What is a Wind Turbine in Real Life?

To understand the code and charts, you must first understand the giant machine sitting out in the ocean or on top of a mountain. A utility-scale wind turbine is not just a fan; it is a multi-megawatt power plant on a 100-meter tower.

  • The Rotor & Blades: Massive blades (50 to 80 meters long) catch the wind. Because of their immense weight, they spin slowly—typically only 10 to 18 Revolutions Per Minute (RPM).
  • The Low-Speed Shaft: Connects the rotating blades into the nacelle (the bus-sized housing at the top).
  • The Gearbox (The Most Vulnerable Component): A standard electrical generator needs to spin at 1,500 to 1,800 RPM to produce 50Hz/60Hz AC electricity. The gearbox multiplies the speed by nearly 90 to 100 times. This creates gigantic mechanical stresses and intense friction on bearings and gear teeth.
  • The High-Speed Shaft (HSS): Spins at 1,500+ RPM inside precision steel bearings. If lubrication fails, metal-on-metal friction generates extreme heat within hours.
  • The Generator: Converts mechanical rotation into electricity using electromagnetic induction. It contains copper windings (the stator) and bearings at both ends (Drive-End DE, and Non-Drive-End NDE).
  • The Transformer: Takes electricity produced at ~690 Volts and steps it up to ~33,000 Volts so power can travel along underwater or underground power lines without massive electrical heat loss.
  • The Hydraulic Group: High-pressure oil pumps that physically twist ("pitch") the blades to catch optimal wind or feather them to a stop in dangerous hurricane conditions.
💡 The Core Mechanical Bottleneck
Over 60% of catastrophic wind turbine downtime is caused by Gearbox, Generator, and Transformer breakdowns. When a steel bearing inside a gearbox begins to wear, it sheds microscopic metal flakes. Those flakes grind the gears, causing runaway friction and heat until the entire assembly violently seizes.

1.2 What is SCADA Telemetry?

SCADA stands for Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition. It is the turbine's digital nervous system.

Instead of hiring technicians to sit inside the turbine 24/7, digital sensors are attached to bearings, oil pipes, gearboxes, and generators. Every 10 minutes, the computer logs:

  • Average temperature of every component (Gearbox bearing, oil, generator, transformer, ambient air).
  • Rotational speeds (RPM) of the rotor and generator.
  • Electrical power (Active Power in Kilowatts kW, Reactive Power in kVAR).
  • Environmental factors (Wind speed in m/s, nacelle ambient air temperature).

2.4 Data Cleaning Hygiene: Why We Strictly Forbid "SMOTE" and Leaks

🎓 Lecturer Trap #1: "Why didn't you use SMOTE to balance the classes?"
Your Answer: "Because SMOTE works by drawing straight mathematical lines between data points in multi-dimensional space. In a physical machine, this creates physically impossible thermodynamic hallucinations! For example, SMOTE might invent a row where a generator produces 2,000 kW while wind speed is 0 m/s, or where a bearing is 80°C while ambient is 0°C and power is 0. A model trained on fake physics fails catastrophically in the real world. We kept 100% genuine sensor data and handled class imbalance using cost-sensitive decision thresholds and scale weights."
⚠️ Lecturer Trap #2: "What is Target Leakage in SCADA, and how did you prevent it?"
Your Answer: "In the raw dataset, there is a column called status_type_id. Whenever a turbine suffers a catastrophic breakdown, its internal safety relay flips this code to 3 or 4 ('Emergency Trip'). If a machine learning model is allowed to see that column, it achieves 99.9% accuracy—not because it predicted anything, but because it simply looked up whether the turbine was already broken! That is called Target Leakage. We strictly stripped out all status codes so our model must predict failure purely from subtle, early thermodynamic changes."

3. Physics-First Feature Engineering

3.1 Why Raw Sensor Temperatures Lie (The Seasonality Trap)

If you feed raw temperatures (e.g., sensor_11_avg = 65°C) directly into a machine learning algorithm, it will make terrible mistakes because of weather seasonality:

  • In Summer (July noon): Ambient air outside is 38°C. A healthy gearbox naturally runs at 62°C simply because the cooling air is warm. The model screams "ALARM!" (False Positive).
  • In Winter (January night): Ambient air outside is -5°C. A failing gearbox with extreme bearing friction is running at 55°C. The raw temperature looks mild, so the model ignores it. The bearing destroys itself hours later! (False Negative).

3.2 The Physics Solution: Thermodynamic Differentials (ΔT)

We engineered Thermodynamic Temperature Differentials by subtracting ambient temperature from every component:

delta_T_gearbox     = sensor_11_avg - sensor_0_avg
delta_T_oil         = sensor_12_avg - sensor_0_avg
delta_T_gen_de      = sensor_13_avg - sensor_0_avg
delta_T_transformer = sensor_39_avg - sensor_0_avg
delta_T_hydraulic   = sensor_42_avg - sensor_0_avg

What ΔT reveals: ΔT isolates purely the internally generated heat caused by mechanical friction and electrical resistance. Whether it is a blazing summer day or freezing winter night, a healthy gearbox has ΔT ≈ 20°C to 25°C. When a bearing fails, ΔT creeps up to 45°C+. The weather effect is completely eliminated!

3.3 Capturing Creeping Friction: Causal Rolling Trends (6-Hour Window)

Mechanical wear does not happen in a single 10-minute snapshot. Metal spalls, fatigue cracks, and lubricant breakdown cause slow, creeping heat over hours.

We engineered 6-hour (36-step) moving averages: roll_mean_gearbox_6h, roll_mean_trans_6h, and roll_mean_hydr_6h.

💡 The "Causal / Backward" Rule
We computed moving averages with align = "right". This means each calculation looks strictly backward into the past 6 hours. If you use a centered moving average, the calculation looks into the future, which is impossible in real-world deployment!

3.4 Electromechanical & Aerodynamic Indicators

  • Gear Ratio Proxy: gear_ratio_est = generator_rpm / rotor_rpm. Under normal locked operation, this ratio is a fixed mechanical constant (~90 to 100). If generator slip rings decouple or gear teeth strip, this ratio becomes erratic.
  • Betz Aerodynamic Power Proxy: power_coefficient = active_power / (wind_speed ^ 3). According to fluid dynamics (Betz Law), the kinetic energy in wind scales with the cube of wind velocity ($v^3$). If wind is blowing hard but power drops, something is aerodynamically or mechanically choking the turbine.

4.2 The Golden Standard: Leave-One-Turbine-Out Group Cross-Validation

🎓 Lecturer Trap #3: "How did you validate your model?"
Your Answer: "We rejected the standard random 80/20 train/test split because time-series SCADA data suffers from strong spatial and temporal autocorrelation. If you randomly split rows, consecutive 10-minute records from the exact same turbine end up in both train and test sets, artificially inflating accuracy to a fake 99%.

Instead, we enforced Leave-One-Turbine-Out Group Cross-Validation (Group K-Fold by Asset ID). When evaluating on Turbine #21, its entire operational history was locked away. The model trained purely on Turbines 11, 12, 13, and 14. This proves that our model works on a brand-new, unseen physical windmill!"

4.3 The Asymmetric Cost Curve & Why Threshold is 0.35 (Not 0.50)

Every standard machine learning tutorial teaches students to use threshold = 0.50 (if probability ≥ 0.50, sound the alarm). In industrial wind energy, a 0.50 threshold is financially reckless.

  • Cost of False Negative ($C_{FN}$): Missing a catastrophic gearbox breakdown = $150,000.
  • Cost of False Positive ($C_{FP}$): Sounding an alarm that turns out to be nothing = $3,500 (a quick visual inspection).

Because missing a failure is 43 times more expensive than a false inspection, our objective function is not standard classification accuracy; it is Minimum Total Financial Loss. When you plot expected financial loss against the alarm threshold, the optimal bottom of the U-curve sits between 0.30 and 0.35. That is why our default decision threshold is set to 0.35.

5. Explainable AI: Demystifying SHAP & LIME

5.1 Why Domain Engineers Reject Black Boxes

Imagine walking into a wind farm control room and telling a veteran mechanical engineer: "The neural network outputs 0.88, so you must shut down this $5 Million turbine and charter a boat."

The engineer will laugh and ignore you. Why? Because they cannot justify spending money without knowing which specific component is breaking. Is it the gearbox? The transformer? A broken wire? Or did a sensor just get unplugged?

5.2 TreeSHAP: Exact Game-Theoretic Credit Assignment

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is grounded in Lloyd Shapley's Nobel Prize-winning game theory. It treats each sensor feature as a "player" in a cooperative game and calculates its exact marginal contribution to the final risk score.

Risk Score = Base Average Risk + Sum(SHAP Value of Feature i)
  • Positive SHAP (+): Pushes the probability UP towards an anomaly alarm (e.g., temp_gearbox_bearing: +0.9069).
  • Negative SHAP (-): Pulls the probability DOWN towards safe, healthy operation (e.g., temp_generator_stator: -0.1520).
  • Global SHAP: Ranks which features matter most across the entire wind farm. In our model, differential temperatures (ΔT) and 6-hour rolling trends occupy the top 5 ranks.
  • Local SHAP: Produces a personalized "waterfall" for a single specific alarm, proving exactly why that turbine was flagged.

5.3 LIME: Local Interpretable Surrogate Rules

While SHAP provides exact mathematical contributions, LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) translates complex tree splits into simple, human-readable "if-then" rules around that specific moment:

✅ Example LIME Rulebook for Event 72 (Gearbox Failure)
IF temp_gearbox_bearing > 58.5°C AND gear_oil_temp > 52.0°C AND delta_T_gearbox > 35.0°C THEN Risk = Anomaly (Weight: +0.78)

8. Lecturer Defense FAQs: 25 Bulletproof Answers

Category A: Dataset & Preprocessing

Q1: Where does this dataset come from?
A: "It comes from the CARE_To_Compare European benchmark published in 2023. Wind Farm A consists of 5 onshore turbines from EDP Open Data in Portugal, while Wind Farms B and C comprise 31 offshore turbines in the German North and Baltic Seas."

Q2: Why are readings averaged every 10 minutes instead of millisecond high-frequency vibration?
A: "High-frequency vibration (10 kHz) produces terabytes of data that cannot be streamed over satellite or cellular links from offshore turbines. Standard wind industry SCADA aggregates sensors into 10-minute statistics (mean, min, max, std). Our model proves that thermodynamic drifts in 10-minute SCADA are sufficient for weeks of advance notice."

Q3: Why did you forbid SMOTE oversampling?
A: "SMOTE creates synthetic points by linear interpolation in feature space. In physical machines, this creates impossible thermodynamic states (e.g. producing 2 MW with zero wind, or a bearing hotter than ambient without generating heat). Real industrial physics demands genuine sensor telemetry."

Q4: What is Target Leakage in SCADA?
A: "When a turbine fails, its internal controller writes status codes like status_type_id == 3 ('Emergency Shutdown'). If an ML model sees that column, it cheats by checking if the turbine is already shut down. We purged all status codes to force the model to detect failures purely from early thermodynamic changes."

Q5: How did you handle missing sensor values?
A: "We used Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) within each turbine's continuous time series. This respects temporal ordering without introducing lookahead bias."

Category B: Feature Engineering & Physics

Q6: Why are raw temperatures insufficient?
A: "Because of seasonal weather bias. In summer at 38°C ambient, a healthy bearing runs at 62°C. In winter at -5°C ambient, a destroyed bearing might only reach 55°C. Raw temperatures cause false alarms in summer and missed failures in winter."

Q7: How does ΔT solve seasonality?
A: "ΔT subtracts ambient temperature from the component temperature (ΔT = T_component - T_ambient). This isolates purely the internally generated frictional and electrical heat, providing an invariant indicator across all seasons."

Q8: Why 6-hour moving averages and not 10 minutes?
A: "SCADA telemetry has short-term turbulence noise. Mechanical bearing degradation causes slow, creeping heat over hours. A 6-hour rolling average smooths momentary spikes while exposing true thermodynamic degradation."

Q9: How did you prevent lookahead leakage in rolling averages?
A: "By strictly using backward alignment (align = 'right'). Every rolling window only looks at past timesteps, matching real-world edge deployment."

Q10: What does the Gear Ratio feature measure?
